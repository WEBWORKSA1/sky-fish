Largemouth Bass Fishing: Seasons, Lures & Patterns

By the Sky.Fish Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-309 min readSpecies

Largemouth bass are the most popular freshwater game fish in North America for good reason. They live in almost every kind of water, from farm ponds to huge reservoirs, they hit lures aggressively, and they fight hard. They are also predictable once you understand that almost everything they do follows water temperature, cover and food. Learn the seasonal cycle and you will know where to start looking any month of the year.

Understanding Largemouth Bass

Largemouth are ambush predators. They hold near cover such as weeds, wood, docks, rocks and drop-offs, and wait for prey to come close. Their diet includes baitfish, sunfish, crayfish, frogs and almost anything else that fits in their oversized mouth. That mouth, which extends past the eye when closed, is the easiest way to tell a largemouth from a smallmouth or spotted bass.

Because they are cold-blooded, bass become sluggish in cold water and most active in warm water, roughly from the mid-60s to low 80s °F (about 18 to 28 °C). They also prefer to avoid bright, direct light, which is why they hug shade on sunny days and roam more under clouds. Our species guide covers how largemouth compare with smallmouth and spotted bass.

Spring: Pre-Spawn, Spawn and Post-Spawn

Pre-spawn

As water warms from the 40s into the 50s °F (roughly 7 to 15 °C), bass begin moving from deep winter areas toward spawning flats. They stage along the first breaks, points and channel edges leading into shallow bays, particularly on the north side of a lake in the northern hemisphere, which gets more sun and warms first. Pre-spawn is one of the best times of year to catch big females, which are feeding heavily.

Lures that work: suspending jerkbaits, lipless crankbaits ripped through emerging weeds, shallow crankbaits along rocky banks, and jigs dragged on transition areas.

Spawn

Spawning typically begins when water temperatures reach the low to mid 60s °F (about 16 to 19 °C), though this varies with latitude and local conditions. Males fan out nests in firm bottoms on shallow, protected flats, and females lay eggs and move off. Males stay to guard the nest and later the fry.

Fishing for nesting bass is legal in many places but closed during the spawn in others. Check local regulations before targeting bass in spring. Where it is legal, many anglers release spawning fish quickly and close to the nest so the male can return to guard it.

Post-spawn

After spawning, females recover in slightly deeper water near the flats and can be hard to catch for a week or two. Males guarding fry stay shallow and aggressive. As water warms further, a strong topwater bite often develops early and late in the day, especially around bluegill spawning areas.

Summer Patterns

Once water is warm, bass split into two broad groups. Some stay shallow in thick cover where there is shade, food and oxygen: matted vegetation, docks, laydowns and overhanging trees. Others move offshore to deeper structure such as ledges, humps, points and deep weed lines, often relating to the thermocline in lakes that stratify.

  • Early and late: Topwater baits such as walking baits, poppers and hollow-body frogs around shallow cover.
  • Midday shallow: Punch heavy weights and soft plastics through vegetation mats, or skip soft plastics under docks.
  • Midday deep: Deep-diving crankbaits, football jigs, Carolina rigs and big worms on offshore structure.
  • Night: In hot weather, dark spinnerbaits, big worms and black jigs can be very effective after dark where night fishing is permitted.
Pro tip: In summer, if you find one bass on an offshore spot, fish it thoroughly from several angles. Deep summer bass often group up, and the first fish is frequently a sign there are more nearby.

Fall and Winter

Fall

As water cools, baitfish move into the backs of creeks and bays, and bass follow them. Fall bass can be scattered and on the move, so cover water with spinnerbaits, squarebill crankbaits, lipless crankbaits and topwater. Look for bait on the surface or on your electronics, and fish where the bait is. Feeding is often strong as bass bulk up before winter.

Winter

In cold water, bass slow down and move to deeper, more stable water near steep breaks, channel bends and deep rock. They still eat, but less often and not far. Slow presentations work best: suspending jerkbaits with long pauses, blade baits hopped off the bottom, small jigs and finesse plastics. On sunny afternoons, bass may move up onto shallower rock that has warmed slightly. In the north, where ice forms, largemouth can be caught through the ice; see our ice fishing safety guide before heading out.

Line choice matters too. Fluorocarbon is popular for jigs, plastics and crankbaits because it sinks and is low visibility; braid is preferred for frogs and heavy cover. Our fishing line guide explains the trade-offs.

How Weather Changes Bass Behaviour

Bass are strongly influenced by weather, especially when shallow. Overcast skies and a bit of wind let them roam and chase, so moving baits shine. Bright, calm, post-frontal days push them tight to cover, where slow and accurate presentations work better. A slowly falling barometer ahead of a front often triggers the best feeding of the week, while the first day after a hard cold front is often the toughest. Our barometric pressure guide covers how to adjust, and the Sky.Fish forecast shows the hourly bite score for your water.

  1. Cloudy and windy: Spinnerbaits, crankbaits, bladed jigs on wind-blown banks.
  2. Sunny and calm: Shade, docks, thick weeds; Texas rigs, jigs and finesse plastics.
  3. Before a front: Fish fast and cover water; bass are often aggressive.
  4. After a front: Downsize, slow down and fish tighter to cover.

Handling and Releasing Bass

Bass can be lifted by the lower lip, but support the body of larger fish horizontally rather than hanging them vertically by the jaw. Keep fish wet, limit time out of water and release them gently. In hot weather, fish caught from deep water may struggle; quick handling helps. Regulations on keeping bass vary widely, so check local size and bag limits.

FAQ

What is the best time of day to catch largemouth bass?

Early morning and the last couple of hours before dark are reliable, especially in summer. Overcast days can produce well all day. In cold months, the afternoon is often better because the water is at its warmest.

What is the best all-round bass lure?

A Texas-rigged soft plastic worm or creature bait is hard to beat. It works in almost any season, depth and type of cover, and it is inexpensive and easy to fish.

At what water temperature do bass spawn?

Largemouth typically spawn when water reaches the low to mid 60s °F (about 16 to 19 °C), but timing varies by region and conditions. Some regions close bass season during the spawn, so check local regulations.

Why can't I catch bass after a cold front?

Post-frontal conditions bring bright skies, rising pressure and cooler water, which make bass hold tight to cover and less willing to chase. Slow down, use smaller baits and make accurate casts to cover.
