Trout Fishing Guide: Streams, Lakes & Stocked Ponds

By the Sky.Fish Editorial TeamUpdated 2026-10-309 min readSpecies

Trout live in some of the most beautiful water anywhere, and they reward anglers who pay attention. They are fussy about temperature, wary of shadows and noise, and quick to inspect a bait and refuse it. That sounds discouraging, but it also makes them predictable. Once you understand what trout need, which is cold, clean, oxygen-rich water, food delivered by current and a place to hide, you can find them in a creek, a deep lake or a stocked town pond.

The Main Trout Species

  • Rainbow trout: The most widely stocked trout. Found in streams, rivers and lakes; known for acrobatic jumps. Sea-run rainbows are called steelhead.
  • Brown trout: Wary, often nocturnal as they grow larger, and more tolerant of slightly warmer water than other trout. Big browns frequently feed on other fish.
  • Brook trout: Technically a char. Prefer very cold, clean water and are often found in small headwater streams and northern lakes.
  • Cutthroat trout: Native to western North America, identified by red or orange slashes under the jaw.
  • Lake trout: Also a char. Live in deep, cold lakes and spend summer well below the surface.

Our species pages cover identification and habitat for each in more detail.

Water Temperature: The Key to Trout

Trout are coldwater fish. Most species feed most actively when water is roughly 50 to 65 °F (10 to 18 °C), with brook trout preferring the cooler end and browns tolerating a bit more warmth. In very cold water, trout are sluggish and feed in short windows, often in the warmest part of the day.

When water gets warm, trout become stressed. Warm water holds less oxygen, and a fish that is fought hard in warm conditions may not recover after release. Many experienced anglers stop fishing for trout once water approaches about 68 °F (20 °C), or switch to early mornings when water is coolest. Some jurisdictions issue voluntary or mandatory closures during heat waves, so check local notices.

Pro tip: Carry a small stream thermometer. Taking the water temperature takes ten seconds and tells you whether to fish fast or slow, shallow or deep, and whether it is responsible to fish for trout at all that day.

Fishing for Trout in Streams and Rivers

Reading the water

Stream trout face upstream and wait for current to bring food to them. They want to hold where they can rest out of the strongest flow while watching faster water nearby. Look for:

  • Riffles: Shallow, broken water that is rich in oxygen and insects. Trout feed here, especially in low light.
  • Runs: Deeper, moderately fast water below riffles. Often the most productive water in a stream.
  • Pools: Slow, deep sections where trout rest. Larger fish often hold here, especially at the head where current enters and the tail where it speeds up.
  • Seams: The line where fast water meets slow water. Trout sit on the slow side and dart into the fast side to feed.
  • Cover: Boulders, undercut banks, logs and overhanging vegetation all provide shelter.

Approach and presentation

Trout spook easily. Move slowly, keep a low profile, avoid wading through water you have not fished yet and stay out of the sun's line so your shadow does not fall across the water. In most cases, work upstream so you approach fish from behind.

The goal is a natural drift. Food in a stream moves at the speed of the current, so a bait or lure that drags unnaturally is often ignored. Cast slightly upstream and let your bait tumble down with the flow, keeping just enough tension to detect a bite.

What to use

  • Small inline spinners: Cast across and slightly upstream, then retrieve just fast enough to make the blade turn.
  • Small spoons: Good in larger, deeper rivers.
  • Drifted bait: A worm or other natural bait drifted with a split shot, where bait is legal.
  • Flies: Nymphs below the surface, dry flies during hatches and streamers for larger trout. See our fly fishing for beginners guide to get started.

Light line is important in clear water. Four to six lb test monofilament or fluorocarbon on an ultralight or light spinning rod is a common setup.

Fishing for Trout in Lakes

In lakes, trout follow temperature and food through the seasons.

Spring and fall

After ice-out and in autumn, surface water is cool, and trout often cruise shallow shorelines, inlets, and points. This is the easiest time for shore anglers. Cast spoons, spinners and small minnow-imitating lures, or fish bait near the bottom off points and drop-offs.

Summer

As the surface warms, trout move deeper to cooler water, often near or below the thermocline. Shore fishing becomes harder. Boat anglers troll spoons or minnow-imitating lures at depth, using weights, downriggers or lead-core line, and electronics help locate the depth where fish are holding. Early and late in the day, trout may move shallower to feed.

Inlets and outlets

Streams flowing into a lake bring cool, oxygenated water and food, and are trout magnets in warmer months. Check regulations, as some inlet areas have special seasons or closures to protect spawning fish.

Stocked Ponds and Put-and-Take Waters

Many fisheries agencies stock hatchery-raised trout into ponds, lakes and streams, and often publish stocking schedules online. Freshly stocked trout are a great opportunity for beginners and kids.

  1. Check the schedule. Fishing is usually best in the days after stocking, before fish disperse or are harvested.
  2. Start near the stocking site. Fish often stay near where they were released at first.
  3. Use simple rigs. Floating dough bait on a small hook with a sliding sinker lets the bait float just off the bottom. Worms under a bobber, small spinners and small spoons also work well.
  4. Watch for cruising fish. Stocked trout often swim in schools near the surface or along the shoreline.
  5. Know the limits. Put-and-take waters often allow harvest, but bag limits still apply.

Hatchery fish get more cautious within days of being fished over. If the bite slows, downsize your hook and line, and try small lures instead of bait.

Timing, Weather and Regulations

Low light is prime time for trout. Early morning, evening and overcast days usually produce the best results, and insect hatches often happen in the evening in summer. A slight rise and colour in a stream after rain can trigger feeding, but a big, muddy flood usually makes fishing tough until the water clears. For more on how weather affects the bite, see our guide to wind, clouds and rain.

Trout fisheries often have more detailed regulations than other species. Before fishing, check local rules for season dates, artificial-lure-only or fly-only sections, barbless hook requirements, catch-and-release waters, size limits and bait restrictions. These rules exist to protect wild fish, and they vary from one stretch of river to the next.

When releasing trout, keep them in the water, wet your hands, and remove hooks quickly. Our catch-and-release guide explains best practice in detail.

FAQ

What is the best bait for trout?

For stocked trout, floating dough bait and worms are very effective. For wild trout in streams, natural drifts with worms, small spinners or flies that match local insects tend to work best. Always check whether bait is permitted on the water you are fishing.

What size line should I use for trout?

Four to six lb test is a good general range for streams and ponds. In very clear water or for wary fish, lighter line can draw more strikes. Heavier line may be needed for larger lake or river trout.

When is the best time of day to catch trout?

Early morning and evening are usually best, along with overcast days. In cold weather, the warmest part of the afternoon can be most productive.

Is it safe to catch and release trout in summer?

It depends on water temperature. When water approaches about 68 °F (20 °C), trout are stressed and may not survive release. Fish early in the morning, land fish quickly, or target other species during heat waves.

Fishing involves risk. Always check local regulations, licence requirements, weather and water conditions before heading out. See our disclaimer.
