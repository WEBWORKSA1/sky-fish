Releasing a fish is not the same as releasing it in good shape. A fish that swims away can still die later from exhaustion, injury, or a damaged slime coat. The good news is that the habits that protect released fish are simple, cost little, and quickly become second nature. Most of them come down to three things: the right hook, a short fight, and as little time out of the water as possible.

Before You Cast: Gear Choices

Good releases start before a fish is hooked.

Hooks

  • Circle hooks with natural bait: Circle hooks are designed to slide to the corner of the fish's mouth and hook there, rather than in the throat or gut. Do not set the hook with a hard sweep; reel steadily and let the rod load. Some fisheries require circle hooks for certain species or methods.
  • Barbless or pinched barbs: Flattening the barb with pliers makes unhooking faster and less damaging. Keep steady pressure during the fight and you will lose very few fish. Some waters require barbless hooks.
  • Single hooks on lures: Replacing treble hooks with single hooks reduces injury and handling time, especially on fish with soft mouths or small heads.

Tackle strength

Line that is too light for the target species forces long fights that exhaust fish. Use tackle heavy enough to land fish reasonably quickly. Our fishing line guide can help you match line strength to the fish you are targeting.

Handling tools

  • A net with a rubber or coated mesh, which is gentler on scales and slime than knotted nylon
  • Long-nose pliers or forceps for removing hooks
  • Line cutters for deeply hooked fish
  • A descending device if you fish deep water, where it is useful or required

Fighting and Landing the Fish

A long fight builds up lactic acid and exhausts a fish's energy reserves, which can reduce its chance of survival after release. Apply firm, steady pressure, use the rod to steer the fish, and bring it in without excessive delay.

When landing, keep the fish in the water if you can. A net helps you control a fish quickly without chasing it around the boat or bank. Avoid dragging fish over rocks, sand or dry boat decks.

Pro tip: Have your pliers, camera and measuring tool ready before you land the fish. Most of the air exposure in a release comes from searching for tools while the fish waits.

Handling the Fish

A fish's skin is covered by a protective slime coat that helps defend against infection. Dry hands, towels, gloves and boat carpet can strip it away.

  1. Wet your hands before touching the fish.
  2. Keep your fingers out of the gills. Gills are delicate and bleed easily.
  3. Do not squeeze. Hold the fish gently but firmly. Pressure on the belly can injure internal organs.
  4. Support larger fish horizontally. Hold one hand near the tail and support the belly with the other. Hanging a heavy fish vertically by the jaw or gill plate can strain its jaw and internal organs.
  5. Keep it low. Handle fish over the water or over a soft, wet surface, so a dropped fish is not injured.

Lip gripping works for some species, such as bass, when done carefully. Even then, support the body of a large fish rather than letting it hang.

Unhooking

Remove the hook quickly and gently. Grip the hook shank with pliers and back it out the way it went in. If you pinched the barb, it should slide free easily.

Deeply hooked fish

If the hook is deep in the throat or gut, cut the line as close to the hook as you can and release the fish. Pulling or digging for a deep hook often causes more harm than leaving it in, and some fish are able to shed a hook left in place.

Fish that are bleeding

A fish that is bleeding heavily from the gills is unlikely to survive. If it is legal to keep, harvesting it may be the most responsible choice. If it is not legal to keep, release it as directed by local regulations.

Air Time and Photos

Out of the water, a fish cannot breathe. The longer it is out, the greater the stress, especially after a hard fight or in warm weather.

  • Keep fish in the water while unhooking when possible.
  • If you want a photo, have your camera ready, lift the fish briefly, then return it to the water.
  • If you need to measure the fish, use a wet measuring board or tape that is ready before the fish comes out of the water.
  • Skip the scale. Hanging a fish from a scale puts its full weight on the jaw. A quick length and girth measurement lets you estimate weight later.

Handling needs differ between species. Fish with sharp teeth, spines or sharp gill plates need different grips than bass or trout, so check the notes for your target fish in our species guides.

Reviving and Releasing

Place the fish in the water upright. In a river, face it into a gentle current so water flows over its gills naturally. In still water, hold it gently and move it slowly forward if needed. Avoid pushing it rapidly back and forth, which can push water the wrong way across the gills.

Let the fish swim away on its own. When it can hold itself upright and kick strongly out of your hands, it is ready. Releasing a fish too soon can cause it to sink or be caught by a predator.

Special Situations

Warm water

Fish are more stressed when water is warm because warm water holds less oxygen. Cold-water species such as trout are particularly sensitive. In warm conditions, keep fights shorter, minimize handling, or fish early in the morning when water is cooler. Some trout waters have voluntary or mandatory restrictions during periods of high water temperature.

Deep-water fish and barotrauma

Fish caught from deep water can suffer from barotrauma, when gas in the swim bladder expands as they are brought up. Signs include a bloated belly, bulging eyes, or the stomach protruding from the mouth. These fish may not be able to swim back down on their own. A descending device can return them to depth. Venting, where a hollow needle releases gas from the swim bladder, is controversial and is regulated differently by location. Learn the proper technique and follow local guidance. Some fisheries require anglers to carry a descending device or venting tool.

Ice fishing

In freezing air, fish can suffer damage to their eyes and gills quickly. Keep them in the water or release them right away. See our ice fishing guide for more on fishing in cold conditions.

FAQ

Do fish survive after being released?

Many do, especially when handled quickly and carefully. Survival depends on factors such as hook location, fight time, air exposure, water temperature and the depth the fish was caught from.

Should I use a towel to hold a fish?

No. Dry towels and gloves can remove the protective slime coat. Wet your hands instead.

Is it better to cut the line or remove a deep hook?

For a deeply hooked fish, cutting the line close to the hook is usually better than trying to pull it out.

Are there rules about catch and release?

Yes. Regulations can include catch-and-release-only waters, required hook types, limits on handling certain species, and requirements for descending devices. Always check local regulations before fishing.
