Catfish are among the most widely available and hardest-fighting freshwater fish in North America, and they bite when many other species shut down: at night, in muddy water, and in the heat of summer. But "catfish" covers three very different fish. Channel, blue and flathead catfish live in different places, eat different food, and respond to different tactics. Fish for the species in front of you and your catch rate will climb.

Know Your Catfish

Channel catfish

Channel cats are the most widespread and the most common catch. They live in rivers, reservoirs, lakes and small ponds, and many ponds are stocked with them. They have a deeply forked tail, and younger fish often have scattered dark spots. Channel cats are opportunistic feeders that eat insects, crayfish, baitfish, plant material and almost anything they can smell, which is why prepared baits work so well for them.

Blue catfish

Blue catfish are the largest catfish in North America and can grow to enormous sizes. They are native to large river systems in the central and southern United States and have been introduced in other areas. Blues are often confused with channel cats, but they lack spots and have a long, straight-edged anal fin, while the channel cat's anal fin is rounded. Blues favor big rivers and reservoirs with current, and large blues feed heavily on fish such as shad and herring.

Flathead catfish

Flatheads have a wide, flattened head, a protruding lower jaw, a square or slightly notched tail, and mottled brown and yellow coloring. They are predators that prefer live fish, and they hold in heavy woody cover such as logjams, fallen trees and undercut banks. Flatheads are mostly solitary and do much of their feeding at night.

Where to Find Catfish

Catfish relate to the bottom and to areas where food collects. Good starting points include:

  • Deep holes and outside river bends: Fish rest in deeper water and move up to feed nearby.
  • Current breaks: Behind rocks, bridge pilings, wing dams and points, where fish can hold out of the main current and wait for food.
  • Channel edges and drop-offs in reservoirs: Especially near flats where fish move up to feed at night.
  • Woody cover: The top choice for flatheads, and also good for channel cats.
  • Below dams: Tailwaters concentrate baitfish and attract all three species. Follow posted safety rules, as water releases can change conditions quickly.
  • Creek mouths and inflows: Especially after rain, when fresh water brings food and oxygen.

Baits by Species

Channel catfish baits

  • Nightcrawlers
  • Chicken livers
  • Prepared dip baits and punch baits, fished on a dip worm or treble hook
  • Cut bait such as shad or sunfish, where legal
  • Shrimp

Blue catfish baits

Fresh cut bait is the standard. Oily fish such as shad and skipjack herring produce a strong scent trail. Fresh bait generally outperforms old or frozen bait, so many blue cat anglers catch their own with a cast net where permitted.

Flathead catfish baits

Live bait is the best choice for flatheads. Common options include bluegill, green sunfish, bullheads, creek chubs and large shiners. Using game fish as bait is illegal in some areas, and many jurisdictions restrict moving live bait between waters to prevent the spread of invasive species and disease. Check local regulations before you collect or use live bait.

Pro tip: Cut bait loses its scent in the water over time. Rebait regularly with fresh chunks, especially when fishing for blues. A fresh bait in a good spot will often outfish an old one in the perfect spot.

Proven Catfish Rigs

Slip sinker rig

Thread an egg or no-roll sinker on the main line, add a bead, and tie on a swivel. Attach a leader with a hook. The fish can pick up the bait without feeling the sinker. This is the most versatile bottom rig for all three species.

Santee Cooper rig

This is a slip sinker rig with a small peg float added to the leader a few inches above the hook. The float lifts the bait slightly off the bottom, keeping it out of snags and more visible to fish. It is a popular choice for drifting or fishing over uneven bottom.

Three-way rig

A three-way swivel connects the main line, a dropper with a sinker, and a leader with the hook. It holds well in current and is useful for fishing behind structure in rivers.

Slip bobber rig

A slip bobber lets you suspend bait at a set depth. It works well for channel cats in ponds and for presenting live bait near cover.

Tie strong, reliable knots such as the Palomar or improved clinch. See our fishing knots guide for instructions.

Tackle and Hooks

Channel cats can be caught on medium spinning or baitcasting tackle with 12 to 20 lb line. For big blues and flatheads, use heavy rods, large baitcasting reels, and strong line, often heavy mono or braid with an abrasion-resistant leader. Our fishing line guide explains the trade-offs.

Circle hooks are a great choice for catfish, especially when fishing from rod holders. When a fish takes the bait, let the rod load and reel steadily instead of setting the hook hard. Hook size should match your bait: small hooks for channel cats with worms or dip bait, and much larger hooks for big cut or live baits.

Use secure rod holders. A large catfish can pull an unattended rod into the water.

Timing and Seasons

Time of day

Catfish feed at all hours, but night fishing is especially productive, particularly in summer. Flatheads are known for their nighttime activity. Dawn and dusk are also strong periods for all three species.

Seasons

  • Spring: As water warms, catfish move into shallower water to feed. Rivers can be excellent as fish move upstream.
  • Late spring to early summer: Catfish spawn when the water warms into roughly the 70s °F. They seek cavities such as holes in banks, logs and rocks. Fishing can slow during the spawn.
  • Summer: Night fishing shines. During the day, fish often hold in deeper water or shaded cover.
  • Fall: Catfish feed heavily as water cools.
  • Winter: Blue catfish remain active in cold water and can be targeted year-round in many areas, often in deeper water. Channel cats and flatheads slow down considerably.

Weather and water

Rising water after rain often triggers feeding, as it washes food into rivers and creeks. Stable, warm weather is generally good, and changing pressure can create short feeding windows. Check our hourly bite forecast to plan your session, and read our barometric pressure guide for more detail.

FAQ

What is the best bait for catfish?

It depends on the species. Channel cats eat a wide variety of baits, including nightcrawlers, chicken livers and prepared baits. Blue catfish prefer fresh cut bait such as shad. Flatheads prefer live fish.

Is catfish fishing better at night?

Often, yes, especially in summer. Night is prime time for flatheads, and channel and blue catfish also feed well after dark.

How do I tell a blue catfish from a channel catfish?

Check the anal fin, the fin on the underside near the tail. A blue catfish's anal fin has a long, straight edge. A channel catfish's anal fin is rounded. Channel cats may also have dark spots, especially when young.

Should I keep big catfish?

Regulations vary, and many waters have limits on large catfish to protect breeding fish. Some areas also issue fish consumption advisories, especially for larger, older fish. Check local regulations and advisories, and consider releasing trophy fish using good catch-and-release practices.
