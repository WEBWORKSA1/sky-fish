Inshore fishing rewards anglers who think like the tide. Redfish, snook, spotted seatrout and flounder share the same shallow bays, marshes, mangrove shorelines and grass flats, and all four feed hardest when moving water pushes bait past them. Learn where that water goes, where it pinches, and where fish wait for it, and you will catch more fish with fewer casts.

Tides Drive Everything Inshore

In freshwater, weather and light set the schedule. Inshore, the tide is the schedule. Slack water at the top or bottom of the tide is usually the slowest fishing of the cycle, because bait stops moving and predators have no reason to set up on ambush points. As soon as the water starts moving again, fish reposition and begin to feed.

Think about what each phase of the tide does to the bait:

  • Rising tide: Water floods the marsh grass, oyster bars and mangrove roots. Redfish push up onto the shallowest flats and into the grass to root for crabs and shrimp. Snook slide up to the edge of mangrove shorelines.
  • High tide: Fish spread out and can be hard to pin down. Work the back edges of flooded grass, deep mangrove pockets, and docks with water under them.
  • Falling tide: Often the most productive phase. Draining water pulls shrimp, crabs and baitfish out of the marsh through creek mouths, cuts and drains. Predators stack up where that bait funnels out.
  • Low tide: Fish concentrate in the deepest available water: channel edges, holes on the flats, and creek bends.

Tide charts give you the predicted times, but wind can push water in or hold it out for hours, so the actual water level may differ from the chart. Strong, sustained onshore or offshore wind matters as much as the printed numbers. Reading Inshore Structure

Inshore fish relate to anything that breaks current or creates an edge. The more of these features overlap in one place, the better the spot.

Oyster bars and shell

Oyster bars hold crabs, shrimp and small fish, and they create current breaks on both tides. Redfish and seatrout feed along their edges, and flounder lie in the sand just off the shell. Be careful: oyster shell cuts line and skin quickly.

Mangroves and docks

Mangrove shorelines are classic snook and redfish cover, especially points and undercut roots with deeper water nearby. Docks provide shade, barnacle-crusted pilings, and ambush points. At night, dock lights attract baitfish and shrimp, and snook and seatrout often hold in the shadow line at the edge of the light.

Grass flats and potholes

Seagrass beds are seatrout country. Look for sandy potholes and the edges where grass meets sand; fish hold in the grass and ambush prey crossing open bottom. Drifting a flat and casting ahead of the boat is an efficient way to find a school.

Creek mouths, cuts and passes

Anywhere water is forced through a narrow opening, current speeds up and bait gets concentrated. Passes and inlets can hold big snook and redfish, while small marsh drains on a falling tide are often where flounder line up facing into the current.

The Four Target Species

Redfish (red drum)

Redfish are bottom feeders with a strong sense of smell, which makes them forgiving for anglers using natural or cut bait. In shallow water you may see them "tailing" as they root head-down for crabs, or pushing a wake. They are tolerant of a wide range of conditions and are often the most dependable inshore target.

Snook

Snook are ambush predators that hold tight to structure and face into current. They are sensitive to cold water, and many fisheries close or restrict harvest during cold snaps or spawning season. Their gill plates are sharp enough to cut light line, so heavier leaders are standard.

Spotted seatrout

Seatrout have soft mouths, so steady pressure lands more fish than a hard hookset and a horsing fight. They often school by size, so if you catch a small one, moving to a different depth or flat can help you find larger fish.

Flounder

Flounder bury themselves in sand or mud and strike prey that passes overhead. Their bite is often a dull weight rather than a sharp tap. Give a flounder a moment to turn the bait before setting the hook, and keep your offering on or very near the bottom.

Live and Natural Baits

Natural bait catches all four species and is the best choice when you are learning a new area.

  • Live shrimp: The universal inshore bait. Fish it under a popping cork for seatrout, on a light jighead, or free-lined near docks and mangroves.
  • Finger mullet and pilchards: Strong choices for snook and big redfish, especially around passes and mangrove points.
  • Mud minnows and mummichogs: Hardy baits that flounder find hard to refuse. Fish them on a Carolina rig dragged slowly across the bottom.
  • Cut mullet, menhaden or ladyfish: Scent trails pull redfish in on a bottom rig, especially in murky water or on a slow day.
  • Crabs: Small blue or fiddler crabs are prime redfish baits around oysters and pilings.

Use circle hooks with natural baits whenever possible. They tend to hook fish in the corner of the mouth, which makes release easier and is required for some species in some areas. See our guide to catch and release for handling tips that keep released fish healthy.

Lures That Work Inshore

A small box of proven lures covers most inshore situations:

  1. Soft plastic paddle tail or shrimp imitation on a jighead: The most versatile inshore lure. Choose the lightest jighead that keeps you near the bottom in the current you are fishing.
  2. Gold weedless spoon: A redfish staple that casts far, flashes in stained water, and comes through grass without fouling.
  3. Topwater walking bait: Excellent for seatrout, snook and reds at dawn, dusk and on overcast days.
  4. Suspending twitchbait: A good choice for snook along mangroves and for seatrout in cooler water when fish want a slower presentation.
  5. Popping cork with a jig or shrimp: The pop imitates feeding fish and draws seatrout and reds to a bait hanging below.

Tackle and Rigging

A 7-foot medium or medium-light spinning rod with a 2500 to 4000 size reel handles most inshore fishing. Many anglers spool with 10 to 20 lb braid for casting distance and sensitivity, then add a fluorocarbon leader. Around 20 lb leader works for seatrout and redfish in open water, while snook around docks and mangroves often call for 30 lb or heavier. Our fishing line guide explains the trade-offs, and the knot library covers leader connections such as the double uni.

Pro tip: Approach shallow flats quietly. Shut down the outboard well before your spot, drift or pole in, and avoid banging hatches or dropping gear on the deck. In skinny water, noise travels and spooked fish rarely come back on the same tide.

Planning Your Trip

Start with the tide, then refine with weather. A falling tide that lines up with early morning or late evening light, a solunar major or minor period, and moderate wind is about as good as conditions get. Check the solunar calendar for the week, then match the best periods against your tide chart.

Inshore regulations vary widely by state and region, including size slots, bag limits, seasonal closures and gear rules for snook, redfish, seatrout and flounder. Always check current local regulations before you go, and confirm whether you need a saltwater fishing license.

FAQ

What is the best tide for inshore fishing?

Moving water is more important than any specific phase. Many anglers prefer the first few hours of a falling tide, when bait is pulled out of the marsh to waiting fish, but the rising tide can be excellent on shallow flats and grass edges.

Can I catch all four species in one trip?

Yes. In many southern estuaries, redfish, snook, seatrout and flounder overlap. A popping cork over grass, a jig along oyster edges, and a live bait near mangroves can produce all four on a good day.

Do I need a boat to fish inshore?

No. Bridges, piers, jetties, seawalls and wadeable flats give shore anglers access to the same fish. Wading also lets you approach shallow fish quietly. Watch for stingrays by shuffling your feet, and be aware of soft mud and incoming tides.

What color lure should I use?

A simple rule works well: natural colors such as white, silver and translucent shades in clear water, and darker or brighter colors such as root beer, gold or chartreuse in stained water and low light.
